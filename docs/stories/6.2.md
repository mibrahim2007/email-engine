## Story

**As a customer of a tenant, I want replies to land in my existing thread and look like the company, so that the exchange feels normal.**

## Acceptance criteria

1. Outbound messages set `In-Reply-To` and `References` correctly from the inbound thread.
2. Replies appear threaded in Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail — verified manually against real accounts.
3. The tenant signature, logo, and footer are applied and previewable.
4. Both HTML and plaintext parts are generated and consistent.
5. Quoted history is included in the standard, expected format.

---

## AC1 depends on a header Story 2.5 is allowed to throw away

`In-Reply-To` is the `Message-ID` of the message being replied to; `References` is that message's `References` plus its `Message-ID`. Both come from the **inbound** message's headers.

§6.2 gives `messages` a `headers jsonb NOT NULL DEFAULT '{}'`, so the raw headers are retained — good. But Story 2.6 resolves threads using `Message-ID`/`In-Reply-To`/`References` and **stores its conclusion in `thread_key`**, which is a normalised grouping value, not a header chain.

**Threading a reply and grouping a conversation are different problems using the same inputs**, and only the second has a column. A reply composed from `thread_key` threads nothing — mail clients do not know what a `thread_key` is.

So this story reads `headers` on the specific message being replied to. Two consequences worth stating in the story rather than discovering in implementation:

1. **The reply is to a message, not to a conversation.** A conversation with eight messages has eight possible parents, and the right one is the latest **inbound** message — not the latest message, which may be the tenant's own previous reply.
2. **`References` grows and has a practical ceiling.** Long threads produce long headers; the convention is to keep the first, then the last several. Truncating from the *front* breaks the chain and clients fall back to subject matching; truncating in the *middle* is what the convention actually prescribes.

## AC5's "standard, expected format" is the one criterion whose failure Epic 2 already fixed

Story 2.5 strips quoted history on the way in (FR17), keeping it expandable. This story puts quoted history back on the way out — and if the two disagree about format, the product **re-quotes its own stripped output**, so each round trip grows the message and the customer sees the conversation twice.

The formats are asymmetric on purpose and the story should say so: **strip liberally, quote conservatively.** Inbound stripping handles every client's variation because it must; outbound quoting emits exactly one format — the `On <date>, <name> wrote:` attribution followed by `>`-prefixed plaintext, and a `<blockquote>` in the HTML part.

**The test that matters is the round trip**, not either half: compose a reply, feed it back through Story 2.5's parser, and assert the quoted block is recognised and stripped. Neither story owns that test alone, so it is named here — the "whichever ships first is not an owner" rule, applied to a test rather than a lint rule.

## AC4's "consistent" is where a security control gets quietly bypassed

Two parts, HTML and plaintext, generated from one draft. Consistency is stated as a formatting concern and is also a safety one: **an agent edits the draft in Story 5.5's composer, and whatever they typed becomes both parts.**

The HTML part is generated from agent-supplied content, so it is generated HTML, not sanitised HTML. NFR13 and §13.1 cover *inbound* rendering — DOMPurify, sandboxed iframe, strict CSP — and none of that applies here, because this is outbound and the author is trusted.

**The author is trusted; the content may not be.** An agent pasting from a customer's email brings that customer's markup with it. The composer escapes on the way in rather than trusting on the way out, and the plaintext part is generated from the *source* text rather than by stripping tags from the HTML — deriving one part from the other is how a `<script>` becomes literal text in one part and a tag in the other.

## AC3 is the one place the tenant's brand meets a spam filter

Signature, logo, and footer. The logo is an image, and an image is either embedded (CID attachment) or remote-loaded — and §13.1 says the product **blocks remote images by default** on inbound, because they are trackers.

Sending remote-loaded images means the tenant's replies are subject to the same suspicion at the other end, and a reply whose logo does not load looks broken. Embed as a CID part: it renders without the recipient permitting remote content, and it does not act as a read receipt on the customer.

Previewable (AC3) means rendered as the customer will see it, which the composer already needs for Story 5.5.

## Tasks

### Task 1 — Threading headers (AC1)

- [ ] `In-Reply-To` = latest **inbound** message's `Message-ID`; `References` = its chain plus itself.
- [ ] Middle-truncate `References` past the length ceiling; never front-truncate.
- [ ] Read from `messages.headers`, not from `thread_key`.

### Task 2 — Body parts (AC4, AC5)

- [ ] HTML and plaintext both generated from the draft source; **neither derived from the other**.
- [ ] Agent-supplied content escaped on entry to the HTML part.
- [ ] One outbound quoting format, plaintext and `<blockquote>`.

### Task 3 — Branding (AC3)

- [ ] Signature, footer from the persona (Story 5.6). Logo as a **CID part**, not a remote URL.
- [ ] Preview renders what the customer receives.

### Task 4 — Client verification (AC2)

- [ ] Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail, against real accounts. **Manual, and it stays manual** — see Prerequisites.

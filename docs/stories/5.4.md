## Story

**As a support lead, I want the bot to know when to stop, so that it never handles something it shouldn't.**

## Acceptance criteria

1. Escalation triggers on unsupported language, PII above threshold, strong negative sentiment, low confidence, and explicit human requests.
2. An escalated conversation is flagged, sorted up, and raises an in-app notification (FR55).
3. The escalation reason is stated in one plain sentence in the conversation timeline.
4. Admins can configure which triggers are active and their thresholds.
5. Escalation precision against a labeled set meets the agreed threshold.
6. A simulated model-provider outage produces queued drafts and human review, never dropped mail (NFR19), verified by a test that fails the Gateway and asserts the conversation still appears with a stated reason.

---

## AC5's threshold exists — it is PRD §1.4, and nothing links the two

Unlike Story 5.1 AC5, this one is not an open question. **PRD §1.4 sets escalation precision at ≥ 85%** ("flagged for human *and* human agreed"). The epic says "the agreed threshold" and the agreement is three sections away in a success-metrics table nobody drafting a story would think to open.

Cite it. **A threshold that exists and is not cited behaves exactly like one that does not exist** — this is the F3/F8/NFR1 shape again, in the direction nobody checks: not a requirement with no owner, but a *number* with no reference.

**And precision alone is the wrong measure to gate on.** Precision asks "of the ones we flagged, how many deserved it" — optimised by flagging almost nothing. The failure this story exists to prevent is a **missed** escalation, which precision cannot see.

Report **recall alongside it** and let §1.4's 85% stay the precision bar. The asymmetry is the point: a false escalation costs an agent thirty seconds; a missed one sends an automated reply to a furious customer or someone's medical detail. Same reasoning as Story 5.1's per-field weighting.

## AC1's five triggers are not five of a kind

| Trigger | Source | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Unsupported language | classification | deterministic once detected |
| PII above threshold | classification | a count against a configured number |
| Strong negative sentiment | classification | a model judgement |
| Low confidence | Story 5.3 | **whatever AC3's decision makes it** |
| Explicit human request | classification **or** `escalate_to_human` | two paths, one outcome |

The fourth was Story 5.3's open decision and is now settled *(2026-08-07, PRD Q10 option B)*: confidence is **computed groundedness**, so "low confidence" means *few of this reply's claims are backed by a retrieved source* — which is exactly the case the trigger exists for. **Under the self-report it would have been a threshold on a number the model chose, and a model that is confidently wrong never trips it.**

**One consequence for this story: `NULL` confidence escalates.** A draft with no factual claims has no groundedness, and the safe reading of "no signal" is human review, not a pass.

The fifth has two paths and needs one owner. The classifier may detect "let me talk to a person"; the agent may call `escalate_to_human` mid-loop. **Both must latch `requires_human` through the same code path**, or the agent's escalation and the classifier's produce different states — and Story 5.1 already ruled that the latch is only cleared by a human.

## AC4 configures thresholds, which makes them tenant data with a floor

Admins set which triggers are active and at what level. **Some of these must not be disableable.**

PII above threshold and explicit human request are not preferences — a tenant turning off "escalate when the customer asks for a human" has configured the product to refuse the one request it must always honour, and NFR21's GDPR posture does not survive an admin disabling PII escalation to raise deflection.

**Ruling: tone-like triggers are configurable; the two above have a floor.** Sentiment and confidence thresholds move freely. PII and explicit-request can have their *thresholds* tuned and cannot be switched off. State it in the UI rather than silently omitting the toggle — an admin who cannot find a switch assumes a bug; one who reads "this cannot be disabled" learns something about the product.

This is the same class as Story 1.6 AC5: **a setting screen is presentation, and what the pipeline actually honours is enforcement.** The floor lives in the escalation evaluator, not in whether the checkbox renders.

## AC6 is the strongest criterion in the epic, and it needs one more assertion

It came from traceability finding F11 — NFR19's degraded path was required and exercised by nothing. Failing the Gateway and asserting the conversation still appears is exactly right.

**Add: assert no draft row exists, and that the timeline sentence says the model was unavailable rather than that the message needs review.** The distinction matters to the agent reading it — "this needs a human because the customer is upset" and "this needs a human because our AI is down" lead to different actions, and NFR23's "every error surfaced to a user shall be actionable" is about exactly that difference.

`conversation_events.type` has `draft_failed` in its CHECK list (§6.7); the `body` column is the one plain sentence AC3 asks for.

## AC2's "sorted up" is the inbox's problem and it is already built

Story 3.1's list sorts by `last_message_at`; Story 3.2 built the "Needs human" saved view. **This story sets the flag; it does not re-implement sorting or the sidebar.** Naming that is the point of the checklist item about what a story must not rebuild — five of Story 1.3's six original criteria had already shipped.

What this story does own: the flag being set through the latch, and the notification being raised once per escalation rather than once per re-evaluation. **A conversation that stays escalated across three inbound messages produces one notification, not three** — the same deduplication reasoning FR56 applies to mailbox alerts.
